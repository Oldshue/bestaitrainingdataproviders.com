How we researched and ranked AI training data providers

Last updated: November 9, 2026

This page explains how we built the ranking on our home page. We are an independent research site. We did not test, buy from or work with any of these providers, and no provider paid to be listed or ranked.

1. Defining the query

We read 'AI training data providers' as companies that sell human-created or human-verified data for training and evaluating AI models: LLM post-training data (SFT, RLHF, rubrics), evaluations and red teaming, agent RL environments, computer-vision and sensor annotation, robotics data, and off-the-shelf datasets. Pure software tools with no data or workforce offering were left out.

2. Building the candidate list

We started from current 2025 to 2026 reporting on the market (AP, TIME, TechCrunch, CNBC), a World Bank report on AI foundations, Sacra's company profile of micro1, and AIData.Broker's revenue-ranked register. AIData.Broker is a commercial market participant that receives referral fees, so we used it for discovery and scale context only, not as independent verification.

3. Verifying each provider

On November 9, 2026 we read each candidate's official website (homepage plus product, enterprise or pricing pages where available) to confirm what it currently sells, who it serves and whether pricing is published. Claims such as network size, customer counts or accuracy rates are reported as the company's own claims. Where a page returned an error (Labelbox's pricing page and one Turing product page returned 404) or showed little detail, we say so instead of filling the gap.

4. Ranking criteria

We weighed these factors, in roughly this order of importance:

  1. Fit for the core query: how directly the provider serves current model-training needs, especially expert post-training data, evaluations and environments.
  2. Public evidence of quality and impact: published benchmarks, research, named customer case studies and before-and-after training results.
  3. Market standing: reported scale (revenue, valuation, funding) from news and analyst sources, treated as directional only.
  4. Breadth: coverage of modalities, domains and languages, and availability of off-the-shelf data.
  5. Buyer transparency and accessibility: public pricing, self-serve options, samples and pilots.
  6. Risk factors: neutrality or ownership changes, narrow geographic workforce rules, and how much detail the public website gives.

We did not score providers numerically, because public evidence cannot support precise quality scores. The order is an editorial judgment based on the factors above, and the comparison table shows where a lower-ranked provider may be the better fit for a particular use case.

5. Known limitations

  • Most leading providers are private. Their revenue and valuation figures are unaudited and come from different reporting periods.
  • Accuracy, inter-annotator agreement, security practice and worker treatment cannot be measured reliably from public sources.
  • Provider websites are self-reported, and benchmark gains published by vendors are not independently replicated here.
  • The market changes quickly. Treat this list as a snapshot dated November 9, 2026.

6. Updates and corrections

We re-check provider sites and news when we update the list, and we change the 'Last updated' date only when the content has been reviewed. If you spot an error, the fix will be based on public, verifiable sources.
